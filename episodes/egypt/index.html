What was daily life like in ancient Egypt?

The year turned with the river. Egyptians named three seasons: Akhet, the inundation, when the flood covered the fields; Peret, the growing season; and Shemu, the harvest. The flood was a gift, not a disaster, bringing new black soil each year, which the Egyptians called the Black Land, against the Red Land of the desert. Most people were farmers or their families. They lived in mud-brick houses with flat roofs where you might sleep on hot nights, wore mostly linen, kept cats for company and for catching snakes and mice, and played board games like senet, whose boards survive though the rules are lost.

What did ancient Egyptians eat?

Food was simple and sustaining. Emmer wheat and barley became bread and a cloudy beer that was food as much as drink. Onions, leeks, garlic, lentils and chickpeas filled the cooking pots, dates and figs sweetened the day, fish came from the river and fowl from the marshes. The rich ate more meat and finer cakes, and drank wine from vineyards in the delta and the oases. In tomb paintings, banquet guests in white linen wear cones of scented fat on their wigs. Even the pyramid builders left traces of good eating: at Giza, archaeologists found a workers' town with bakeries and cattle bones.

How were the pyramids built?

The first great leap came at Saqqara, where the architect Imhotep raised a stepped stone pyramid for King Djoser. Kings at Meidum and Dahshur then experimented with smooth sides (the Bent Pyramid changes its angle partway up), and at Giza, Khufu's Great Pyramid rose around the middle of the twenty-sixth century BCE, cased in fine Tura limestone with a burial chamber lined in granite from Aswan. There is no surviving instruction manual. The evidence points to organised labour in rotating crews, not a nation of slaves in chains, and to ramps of mud brick and rubble whose exact designs specialists still discuss. Herodotus's tales of forced labour are treated with caution by modern Egyptologists.

Which pharaohs does the episode meet?

Narmer, shown on the Narmer Palette wearing the crowns of both Upper and Lower Egypt. Hatshepsut, who ruled as pharaoh and whose temple records a trading voyage to the land of Punt. Thutmose III, victor at Megiddo. Akhenaten, who raised the sun-disk Aten above the old gods at his new city of Amarna, with Nefertiti beside him. Tutankhamun, a relatively minor king made famous when Howard Carter found his nearly intact tomb in 1922. Ramesses II, who reigned about sixty-six years, built Abu Simbel and made one of the earliest surviving peace treaties with the Hittites. And Cleopatra VII, after whose death in 30 BCE Egypt became a Roman province. Along the way, the tomb-builders of Deir el-Medina stage what many historians call the first recorded labour strike, under Ramesses III, when their grain rations failed to arrive.

What did Egyptians believe about the afterlife?

Death was a hazardous crossing, not an ending. In its fullest form, mummification took about seventy days: organs removed, the body dried with natron and wrapped in linen with amulets, the heart usually left in place for judgment. In the weighing of the heart, Anubis adjusts the scales and Thoth records the result as the heart is set against the feather of Maat. A heart light with truth may enter the Field of Reeds, a perfected Egypt of endless harvests. The spells to help the dead are what modern readers call the Book of the Dead; the papyrus of Ani in the British Museum is a famous example.

How were hieroglyphs deciphered?

The last known hieroglyphic inscription, at Philae, dates to the early fifth century CE. In 1799, soldiers on Napoleon's expedition found the Rosetta Stone at Rashid, carrying one decree in hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek. Because Greek could still be read, it became a key, and in 1822 Jean-François Champollion announced his breakthrough, building on earlier work by Thomas Young and others.
