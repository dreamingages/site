Samurai history for sleep: a calm night in feudal Japan, from the Heian court and the Kamakura shogunate to the Tokugawa peace. This sleep story walks gently through feudal Japan, from the elegant Heian court in Kyoto and the rise of the samurai to the long Tokugawa peace and the Meiji Restoration of 1868, which ended the old military order. It keeps returning to one small picture: mist between the islands, settling over rice terraces after rain. Where historians disagree, the narration says so. It's narrated softly, with no loud sounds, no graphic violence and no ad breaks.

How did feudal Japan begin?

Heian-kyo, the city we now call Kyoto, was founded in 794, and for centuries the emperor and the court remained there. The Heian period, lasting in broad terms until 1185, was an age of poetry, calligraphy and layered silk; Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji there in the early eleventh century. Out in the provinces, though, land needed protection, and local strongmen with mounted retainers, the early bushi, became the warrior class we call samurai. In the Genpei War, from 1180, the Minamoto house defeated its Taira rivals, finally at the sea battle of Dan-no-ura in 1185. Minamoto no Yoritomo then built a warrior government at Kamakura, rewarding loyal warriors with stewardships over estates and provinces: the web of land and military service that later ages would call feudal.

Who was the first shogun, and was there a bushido code?

In 1192 the court granted Yoritomo the title sei-i taishogun, usually shortened to shogun. After his death in 1199, real control passed to the Hōjō, the family of his widow Masako, who ruled as regents. Ideals of loyalty, readiness to die and scorn for luxury deepened in these decades, but the episode is careful: the word bushido, the way of the warrior, was polished by later ages, and in Kamakura times it was not yet a tidy code.

Does kamikaze really mean "divine wind"?

Yes. Under Kublai Khan, the Yuan dynasty of China demanded Japan's submission. In 1274 a fleet struck northern Kyushu around Hakata Bay, and a storm damaged it as it withdrew. In 1281 a far larger force returned, met defensive walls along the shore, and was smashed by a great typhoon. Later memory named these storms kamikaze, divine winds. Historians still weigh weather against Japanese defence, but Japan was not conquered. The episode keeps the medieval meaning, those storms understood as heaven's intervention, separate from the word's much later and tragic reuse.

Who were the three unifiers of Japan?

After Kamakura fell in 1333, the general Ashikaga Takauji set up a rival emperor in 1336, and for nearly sixty years Japan had two imperial courts. The Ashikaga shoguns gave Kyoto the Golden Pavilion, but the Onin War, from 1467, broke their authority and, for many historians, opened the Sengoku period, the age of warring states. Oda Nobunaga won at Okehazama in 1560, entered Kyoto in 1568 and ended the Ashikaga shogunate in 1573, before he was betrayed by Akechi Mitsuhide and died at the Honno-ji temple in 1582. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, a commander of humble origins, built Osaka Castle, ordered sword hunts, and in the 1590s invaded Korea in campaigns that caused immense suffering and failed. Tokugawa Ieyasu won at Sekigahara in 1600, became shogun in 1603 and governed from Edo. When Osaka Castle fell in 1615, the Tokugawa peace had truly begun.

What was life like in feudal Japan?

For most people, the year turned with the rice: flooded fields and seedlings transplanted by hand in spring, weeding in summer, harvest and drying racks in autumn, and mending, spinning and storytelling in winter. Farmers lived in wooden houses with thatched or shingled roofs, often gathered around an irori, a sunken hearth where a kettle hung. Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples stood side by side, and many people moved easily between both. In the tea room, Sen no Rikyu's wabi aesthetic treasured the crooked, the muted and the incomplete.

What did people eat in feudal Japan, and what did samurai eat?

Meals were built around rice when harvests allowed, with barley, millet, vegetables, pickles, miso soup, and fish for those near the coast or rivers. Meat eating was limited by Buddhist custom and by scarcity, though practice varied by region and century, and sake warmed festivals and farewells. The episode doesn't describe a samurai's daily menu, so we won't guess at one. What it does say is that samurai were paid in rice stipends measured in koku, in theory enough to feed one person for a year, and that many lower samurai lived frugally, more like salaried clerks with swords than glittering heroes.

What was sakoku, and how did feudal Japan end?

From the 1630s the shogunate expelled Portuguese traders, suppressed Christianity with cruel force after the Shimabara rebellion, and limited most Western contact to a Dutch trading post on the artificial island of Dejima at Nagasaki. Later scholars called this sakoku, the closed country, a partly misleading term: Chinese and Korean contact continued under regulation, and Dutch learning still brought in medicine and maps. Inside that peace, alternate attendance sent daimyo processions along roads such as the Tokaido, and Basho's poems, kabuki and ukiyo-e prints flourished. In 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry's black ships entered Edo Bay. In 1868 came the Meiji Restoration: the last shogun stepped down, Edo was renamed Tokyo, and the feudal age settled into castles, customs and seasons.
