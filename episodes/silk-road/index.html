What was the Silk Road?

We might picture one great highway, but there never was one. The Silk Road was a web of paths, walked by merchants, monks and messengers for well over a thousand years. Very few people travelled its whole length. Goods moved from hand to hand and oasis to oasis, often on long lines of two-humped Bactrian camels, whose thick, shaggy coats let them bear the cold of a desert winter. Silk travelled even before the road had a name: a felt saddle cloth from the frozen burial mounds of Pazyryk, in the Altai mountains, has a panel of Chinese silk sewn into it, from around the fourth or third century BCE.

Why is it called the Silk Road, and how was silk made?

The name is modern: in 1877 the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen helped make it popular. Silk is far older. Archaeologists in central China have found silk fabric in burials of the Yangshao culture more than five thousand years old. Silk comes from the cocoon of the silkworm, a caterpillar that feeds on mulberry leaves. The cocoons were softened in hot water and the filament carefully reeled off, and a single cocoon could give a thread hundreds of metres long. In 2009, UNESCO recognised Chinese sericulture and silk craftsmanship as part of the world's living heritage.

Who started the Silk Road?

No single person did, but the story often begins with Zhang Qian. Around 138 BCE, Emperor Wu of Han sent him west from Chang'an, near modern Xi'an, to win the Yuezhi as allies against the Xiongnu. He was held by the Xiongnu for about ten years, slipped away, and reached Dayuan, which most historians identify with the Fergana Valley, home of tall horses the Chinese would call heavenly horses. The Yuezhi politely declined the alliance, and he came home about thirteen years after setting out, with only one other man of his company. His reports opened the way: Han missions soon went west every year, and grapes and alfalfa took root in China.

What was traded on the Silk Road?

Silk went west, where the Romans called its makers the Seres. At Begram, north of Kabul, two sealed rooms held Roman painted glass, Indian-style ivories and Chinese lacquered bowls together. Sogdian letters from around 313 speak of silk, musk, pepper and silver. Some of the most important cargo was lighter: Buddhist teachings carried by monks and translators such as An Shigao and Kumarajiva, and learning such as that of the mathematician al-Khwarizmi, whose work gave us the words algebra and algorithm. Not everything was welcome: recent ancient-DNA research suggests the Black Death may have begun in Central Asia before travelling west along the trade routes.

Who were the Sogdians?

From about the fifth to the eighth centuries especially, some of the busiest hands on the road belonged to the Sogdians, from the land around Samarkand and Bukhara. They lived at a crossroads between Persia, India, the steppe and China. Merchant families sent sons and brothers to live in distant towns, Sogdian communities grew up deep into China, and their Eastern Iranian language became a common tongue of trade on the eastern roads. In 1907 Aurel Stein found a bundle of Sogdian letters in a ruined watchtower west of Dunhuang, a mailbag that never reached its destination. Two were written by a woman named Miwnay, left behind in Dunhuang with her daughter and asking her family for help.

Where did Silk Road travellers stop?

Around the Taklamakan desert, caravans followed a ring of oases: Dunhuang with its painted caves, Turfan with its underground karez channels, Kucha, Khotan with its jade, and Kashgar, where the routes met again. In 629 the monk Xuanzang crossed an icy pass of the Tian Shan, which historians usually identify with the Bedel Pass, and came down beside Issyk-Kul in today's Kyrgyzstan, then to the trading town of Suyab. Tash Rabat, a stone caravanserai high in the mountains of Naryn, sheltered travellers for centuries. Under the Mongol peace, William of Rubruck, Marco Polo, Rabban Bar Sauma and Ibn Battuta travelled the roads of Asia, and in 1370 Timur made Samarkand his capital.

What happened to the Silk Road?

Ships had long sailed the monsoon winds of the Indian Ocean; Zheng He's fleets sailed between 1405 and 1433, and in 1498 Vasco da Gama reached India by sea. Little by little the great trade drifted out to sea, many caravan towns grew quiet, and in the Tarim Basin the sands crept over orchards and walls. In 1900, at the Mogao Caves near Dunhuang, the caretaker Wang Yuanlu found a hidden room packed with scrolls, including a printed Diamond Sutra dated 868, the oldest dated printed book known to survive. Since 1994 the International Dunhuang Project has gathered the scattered manuscripts again as photographs anyone can browse, and in 2014 UNESCO listed the Chang'an–Tianshan corridor, including sites in the Chuy Valley of Kyrgyzstan, as World Heritage.
