Ancient Greek history for sleep: a calm journey through ancient Greece, from Minoan Knossos to Sparta, Athens and Alexander the Great. This sleep story walks slowly through about two thousand years of ancient Greece, from the Bronze Age palaces of Crete and Mycenae to Alexander's brief empire and the Hellenistic kingdoms that followed. It keeps returning to one small picture: a harbour light at dusk. Where historians disagree, the narration says so. It's narrated softly, with no loud sounds, no graphic violence and no ad breaks.

Who were the Minoans and Mycenaeans?

On Crete, beginning in the third millennium BCE and flowering especially in the second, a civilisation rose that later scholars named Minoan, after the legendary king Minos. At Knossos and Phaistos, multi-storey palaces held storage jars, workshops and frescoes of dolphins, lilies and figures leaping near bulls. Their script, Linear A, remains mostly undeciphered. On the mainland, from roughly 1600 to about 1100 BCE, Mycenaean centres such as Mycenae, Tiryns and Pylos built walls of enormous stones and beehive-shaped tholos tombs. Their Linear B tablets, deciphered in the 1950s, turned out to be an early form of Greek. Around 1200 BCE and in the decades after, many palace centres collapsed, for reasons still argued.

What was a polis?

After a thinner age that historians often call the Dark Age, the eighth century BCE brought the Greek alphabet, adapted from Phoenician signs with added vowels; the Olympic Games, traditionally dated to 776 BCE, a date scholars treat as conventional; and the Iliad and the Odyssey. Out of this grew the polis. It meant more than a town with walls: it was a community of citizens, a political and religious whole, often with its surrounding farmland. There were hundreds of them. Sparta trained its citizen boys in the agoge, was ruled by two kings side by side, and rested on the labour of helots, an unfree population bound to the land. Athens, after Solon's reforms and the changes of Cleisthenes around 508 or 507 BCE, built what it called demokratia, though women, slaves and resident foreigners had no vote.

What happened in the Persian Wars?

After an Ionian revolt in 499 BCE, a Persian force landed at Marathon in 490 BCE and was beaten by Athenian hoplites and their Plataean allies. Ten years later, under Xerxes, Persia returned. The Spartan king Leonidas and a small force held the pass at Thermopylae for days, and in the narrows of Salamis Greek seamanship broke the Persian navy. In 479 BCE, victories at Plataea and Mycale ended the invasion. Afterward, under Pericles, Athens built the Parthenon, its playwrights wrote for the festivals, and Socrates asked his questions in the Agora until his death in 399 BCE.

What was daily life like in ancient Greece?

Most Greeks lived in the countryside, and the year turned with barley and wheat, the olive harvest in late autumn and the grape vintage. Houses were mud brick or stone with clay-tile roofs, their rooms arranged around a small courtyard. People wore draped linen or wool: the peplos and chiton, the himation cloak, and the shorter chlamys for travel. Each morning might begin with a small offering to Hestia at the household hearth, and city calendars filled with festivals such as the Panathenaia at Athens and the Thesmophoria for Demeter. Athenian silver "owls" travelled far as trusted coins. The episode doesn't look away from slavery: enslaved people worked in households, workshops, fields and the Laurion silver mines.

What did ancient Greeks eat?

Meals were more modest than the banquet stereotype. Barley cakes, wheat bread when possible, olives, cheese, vegetables, figs, and fish for those near the sea formed the base. Meat appeared more often at sacrifices and festivals than as daily fare, and festivals shared out the meat from sacrifices, so even poorer citizens tasted it. Wine was mixed with water in a krater; drinking it unmixed was thought excessive. The symposium, a male drinking party with conversation, music and games, belonged to elite culture. Ordinary evenings were quieter: a lamp, a story, and early sleep after a day in the fields.

How did ancient Greeks sleep, and what were the sleep temples?

In summer, people slept on flat roofs under the stars when heat filled the lower rooms; in winter, a brazier's charcoal was wealth as real as silver. Healing could involve sleep too. It mixed herbal knowledge with temple sleep: at Epidaurus, pilgrims sought Asclepius, hoping for a dream that would guide their cure, and the theatre there belonged to a sanctuary of recovery as much as to drama.

What happened after Alexander the Great died?

After the long Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE) and a restless fourth century, Philip II of Macedon defeated Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea in 338 BCE. His son Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 and died at Babylon in 323 BCE, aged thirty-two. His generals, the Diadochi, fought over his realm, and out of decades of war came the Hellenistic kingdoms: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids across much of Asia and the Antigonids in Macedon. Alexandria grew into a marvel, with the Pharos lighthouse and its great Library, and in Athens Zeno taught Stoicism and Epicurus taught in his Garden. Then came Rome, which destroyed Corinth in 146 BCE. What remained was the alphabet beneath our own letters, and words such as democracy, theatre, history, poetry, museum and school.
