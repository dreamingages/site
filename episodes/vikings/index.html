Viking history for sleep: a calm night in the Viking Age, from longships and the Lindisfarne raid to Dublin, Iceland and Vinland. This sleep story walks slowly through the Viking Age, from about the late eighth century to the middle of the eleventh. It starts on the farms beside the fjords and follows the ships to Britain and Ireland, down the rivers of the east, and out across the North Atlantic to Iceland, Greenland and a brief shore in North America. It is narrated softly, with no loud sounds, no graphic violence and no ad breaks.

Who were the Vikings?

The people we now call Vikings spoke related North Germanic languages, in the lands we know today as Norway, Sweden and Denmark. In Old Norse, a viking could mean a voyage, or a person who went on one, often with raiding in mind. Yet most people in these lands spent their lives farming, trading and looking after their families. Historians still debate how tightly the word should be tied to a few centuries of overseas raiding, and how much ordinary Scandinavian life it should cover. The episode uses it gently, as a doorway into a longer story of northern peoples on the move.

What did Vikings eat?

Most days in the Viking Age turned on farms. People grew barley and rye, cabbage and onions, and kept cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and horses. Food was seasonal and inventive: dried fish, smoked meat, skyr-like dairy, flatbreads, porridge and buttered roots filled the bowls, while ale and mead appeared at feasts. Autumn was the time for slaughter and storage, and in the lean months the stores from that slaughter had to stretch. In winter, people lived close to the smell of wool, smoke and stored fish. Further west, Icelandic woollens and dried fish became goods in North Atlantic trade.

The episode doesn't follow a crew's rations at sea, so we won't guess at them here. What it describes is the larder those crews sailed from, and the duty of hospitality: refusing a traveller without good reason could stain a family's reputation.

How were Viking ships built?

Ships were what made the distances possible. The classic longship was long, narrow and clinker-built: overlapping planks clenched with iron rivets, so the hull flexed with the sea. Shipwrights chose oak when they could and pine when they had to, and caulked the seams with tar and animal hair. A square sail caught the wind, and oars took over when the wind dropped. Broader ships called knarrs carried cargo and settlers. Many voyages were about timber, furs, walrus ivory, silver and trade, not only raiding.

Where did the Vikings go?

The raid later ages treat as a landmark struck the monastery on Lindisfarne, off the Northumbrian coast, in 793. Historians are careful to say it may not have been the very first. In Ireland, Scandinavian ship camps grew into towns, Dublin among them. In England, a force the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle calls the Great Army campaigned from about 865; its size is still debated. Alfred of Wessex defeated a Danish army at Edington in 878, and the Danish leader Guthrum accepted baptism. Place-names ending in -by and -thorpe still mark the old Danelaw, and by 1016 Cnut ruled a North Sea empire linking England and Denmark.

In Francia, a leader named Rollo received lands around the lower Seine that became Normandy. To the east, travellers followed rivers toward Kyiv and Constantinople, which the Norse called Miklagard, and silver coins from the Islamic world flowed north in great quantities. In the Atlantic, settlement of Iceland sped up around 870, and the Althing assembly is traditionally dated to 930. Erik the Red promoted settlement in Greenland around 985 or 986. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, excavations since the 1960s have revealed a Norse site from around the year 1000: firm proof that Scandinavians reached North America.

How did the Viking Age end?

Slowly, and as a tide rather than a single event. Kings in Denmark, Norway and Sweden gathered wider power and took up Christianity. The rune stones at Jelling proclaim Harald Bluetooth's claim to have made the Danes Christian, and Olaf Haraldsson became Saint Olaf after his death at Stiklestad in 1030. Historians often use the death of Harald Hardrada at Stamford Bridge in 1066, less than three weeks before the Battle of Hastings, as a symbolic bookend. As the episode puts it, the age ended partly because the raiders' grandchildren became the kings and dukes of a Christian map.
