This sleep story asks a quiet question: what did people in ancient Mesopotamia do when the sun went down? It walks slowly through the evenings of Sumer, Akkad, Babylon and Assyria, in the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates, using the words people left on clay tablets and the things archaeologists have found in their houses. It keeps returning to one small picture: the moon over the two rivers. Where we can only imagine, the narration says so. It's narrated softly, with no loud sounds, no horror and no ad breaks.

When did the day begin in ancient Mesopotamia?

For the Babylonians, the day began at sunset, and each month began in the evening, when the first thin crescent of the new moon appeared in the western sky. The night was divided into three watches, in Akkadian maṣṣartu: the evening watch, the middle watch and the watch of dawn, when guards changed on the city walls. Scholars later divided the day and night into twelve double hours, called bēru. The Babylonians counted in a system based on sixty, and it is partly because of them that we still have sixty minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle. At sunset, the sun god, Utu to the Sumerians and Shamash to the Akkadians, was thought to pass through the western mountains and travel through the underworld by night.

What were houses like, and did people sleep on the roof?

With little stone or timber in the south, people built with mud brick and reeds. Houses pressed close together around small courtyards, with plain walls and few windows facing the street. Leonard Woolley's excavations at Ur in the 1920s and 1930s uncovered whole streets of houses from the early second millennium BCE; he gave the lanes names like Paternoster Row and believed many houses had upper storeys, which later archaeologists have questioned. Roofs were flat, made of beams, reed matting and packed mud. It seems very likely that families sat on them in the evening, though the ancient texts give only glimpses, and likely that in hot weather many slept outdoors in the courtyard or on the roof.

How did they light their homes, and what did they eat for supper?

Most lamps were small clay dishes with a wick of twisted plant fibre or wool, and the most common lamp oil came from sesame; olive oil was less common, especially in the south. The diet was built on barley, which grew better than wheat in the salty southern soils: flat breads from clay ovens, porridges, soups and, above all, beer. Alongside it came lentils, chickpeas, onions, garlic, leeks, cucumbers, dates, river fish, and milk and cheese from sheep and goats. Three clay tablets at Yale University, from around 1750 BCE, hold instructions for more than two dozen dishes, mostly broths and stews, among the oldest written recipes in the world. We don't know how often ordinary families ate them.

Did people in Mesopotamia drink beer through straws?

Yes. Beer was often thick and unfiltered, so people drank it through long straws, often from a shared jar; a cylinder seal from Ur shows two figures sipping through tall, bending tubes, and a drinking tube of gold and lapis lazuli was buried with Puabi, a woman of high rank, about 4,500 years ago. A Sumerian hymn to the beer goddess Ninkasi, written down around 1800 BCE, praises the brewing, though scholars caution that it is a poem, not a precise recipe. Taverns, kept by a sābītu, appear in the laws of Hammurabi, and in the Epic of Gilgamesh the tavern keeper Siduri gives the grieving hero gentle advice: let your belly be full, let your clothes be clean, and look at the child who holds your hand.

Where did people sleep, and were there lullabies?

Wealthy households had beds with wooden frames strung with cords, sometimes inlaid with ivory or carved with the feet of bulls or lions. Most people slept on a reed mat or woollen blanket, on a beaten earth floor or a low mud-brick bench. Rare and lovely lullabies survive. One Old Babylonian incantation speaks to a newborn who has come out from the house of darkness, asks why it cries, and pleads for sleep to fall on the child as sleep falls on a drinker of wine; a Sumerian lullaby wishes a son sleep, good health and a happy life. Scholars debate whether these were real songs, spells or literary pieces.

What did Mesopotamians believe about dreams?

Dreams could be messages from the gods. Around 2100 BCE, Gudea, ruler of Lagash, recorded on two large clay cylinders, now in the Louvre, a dream that the goddess Nanshe explained as a command to build a temple for the god Ningirsu. Later scholars gathered hundreds of dreams and their meanings into a reference book known in Akkadian as Zaqīqu, sometimes called the Assyrian Dream Book, which survives mostly in copies from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Enkidu interprets his friend's frightening dreams as good signs. To undo a bad dream, some texts describe telling it to a lump of clay and dissolving the clay in water.

Who were the gods of the night?

At Ur the greatest god was the moon, Nanna to the Sumerians and Sin to the Akkadians, imagined as a wise elder with a beard of lapis lazuli sailing a crescent boat. His ziggurat, built by King Ur-Nammu around 2100 BCE, still stands on the plain. His most famous priestess was Enheduanna, a daughter of Sargon of Akkad, traditionally credited with hymns that would make her the earliest author known by name, though some scholars question how many she wrote. Inanna, or Ishtar, was linked with Venus, the evening and morning star. An Old Babylonian Prayer to the Gods of the Night describes a city falling asleep, its bolts drawn and its gods gone to the lap of heaven, while a diviner calls on the stars. Amulets of the spirit Pazuzu were hung to drive away the demon Lamashtu, and little clay dogs were buried under doorways to keep watch.

Who were Babylon's star-watchers?

Scholars recorded thousands of sky omens in a series called Enūma Anu Enlil. The Venus tablet of Ammisaduqa records the risings and settings of Venus over about twenty-one years, and historians still debate the dates it implies. From the middle of the first millennium BCE, Babylonian scholars kept the Astronomical Diaries, nightly records of the sky that also noted the weather, the level of the Euphrates and the price of barley, continuing with gaps for about six hundred years. They learned to predict lunar eclipses, and in 2016 the historian of science Mathieu Ossendrijver published tablets that seem to track Jupiter with a geometric method once thought to have first appeared in medieval Europe, more than a thousand years later. By around the fifth century BCE they had divided the sun's path into the twelve signs of the zodiac. The popular idea that ziggurats were observatories has little direct evidence behind it.

What happened to the cities, and how were the tablets rediscovered?

Nebuchadnezzar II built the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate in the early sixth century BCE, and a reconstruction now stands in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin. No Babylonian text mentions the Hanging Gardens, and some scholars think they were at Nineveh, or partly legend. Nineveh fell in 612 BCE; Cyrus the Great took Babylon in 539 BCE without destroying it. The last datable cuneiform tablet known is an astronomical text from the first century CE, and the cities melted into mounds called tells. In the nineteenth century, Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam, an Iraqi-born archaeologist from Mosul, dug at Nineveh and Nimrud and found Ashurbanipal's library, whose more than thirty thousand tablets and fragments are now in the British Museum. In 1872 George Smith realised that a broken tablet told of a great flood: part of the Epic of Gilgamesh.
