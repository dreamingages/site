This sleep story walks slowly through Byzantium, the eastern Roman Empire, and its capital Constantinople, a city on the water between Europe and Asia that was a capital for more than eleven hundred years. It begins with a small Greek colony on a green headland and ends with the city's last chapter in 1453, and what quietly lived on. It keeps returning to one small picture: water moving between two shores. Where historians disagree, the narration says so. It's narrated softly, with no loud sounds, no graphic violence and no ad breaks.

Why is it called Byzantium?

Greek settlers founded a town on a hilly headland at the southern end of the Bosphorus in the seventh century BCE. Ancient writers usually give a date around 660 BCE, a tradition rather than a certainty, and say the settlers came from Megara, near Athens. They called the town Byzantion. A later story, which the Roman historian Tacitus also tells, credits a founder named Byzas, but it is best heard as a legend. The people of the empire never called themselves Byzantine: they were Romans, in Greek Rhomaioi, and their state was simply the Roman Empire. The word Byzantine was made popular much later, in the 1500s, by the German scholar Hieronymus Wolf.

How did Byzantion become Constantinople?

Constantine the Great won a decisive battle nearby, on the Asian shore, in 324, chose the old town as his new capital in the east, enlarged it, and dedicated it on 11 May 330. People called it Nova Roma, New Rome, and soon Constantinople. He was the first Roman emperor to favour Christianity, and the city grew up with churches beside its pagan monuments. In the early 400s, under Theodosius II, the land walls were built: about six kilometres from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn, a triple line of moat, outer wall and tall inner wall with nearly a hundred towers. Water came from the hills of Thrace through a system that grew to hundreds of kilometres; the aqueduct of Valens still crosses a valley in the modern city. When the last western emperor was set aside in 476, the empire in the east simply carried on.

Who were Justinian and Theodora, and how was Hagia Sophia built?

Justinian, from a farming family in the Balkans, became emperor in 527. Theodora's early life is told mainly by the historian Procopius, whose Secret History historians treat with great caution. Justinian's scholars, led by Tribonian, gathered Roman law into what was later called the Corpus Juris Civilis, which still shapes legal systems today. In January 532 the Nika riot, when the Blues and the Greens of the Hippodrome turned against the emperor, burned the old Hagia Sophia. Theodora's famous speech that royalty makes a good burial shroud comes from Procopius, and historians suspect the words are more his than hers. Within weeks, work began on a new Hagia Sophia, designed by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus and dedicated in December 537: a dome more than thirty metres across, with a ring of forty windows at its base. Procopius wrote that it seemed to hang from heaven by a golden chain. The line about Justinian surpassing Solomon appears only in sources written long afterwards.

What is the Basilica Cistern?

Beneath the streets nearby lies another of Justinian's works, known today as the Basilica Cistern, and in Turkish as Yerebatan Sarnıcı, the sunken palace. Its roof rests on more than three hundred columns, many reused from older buildings, and water from the aqueducts rested there for dry months and sieges. Two column bases are carved with heads of Medusa, set sideways and upside down, perhaps simply because they were reused blocks. Justinian's reign also brought the plague of 541–542, which modern scientists have confirmed was caused by the same bacterium as the later Black Death; how many died is still debated.

What was daily life like in Constantinople?

The episode pictures an ordinary day in the ninth or tenth century, drawn from laws, letters, saints' lives and archaeology rather than one record. Monks were called to prayer by the knocking of a wooden semantron. Bread was the foundation of city life, with grain from Egypt and, after Egypt was lost in the seventh century, from Thrace, Asia Minor and the Black Sea lands. The early tenth-century Book of the Eparch set rules for the guilds: perfume sellers stood near the palace, and tavern keepers had to close and put out their fires at the second hour of the night. The main street, the Mese, ran west through great squares under colonnades. Procopius says monks smuggled silkworm eggs into the empire in hollow canes, and the finest purple silks were reserved for the court; the envoy Liudprand of Cremona complained when customs took his. In the square that now covers the Hippodrome, an Egyptian obelisk brought by Theodosius I and the twisted bronze serpent column from Delphi still stand.

What was Greek fire, and what was Iconoclasm?

In the 630s new armies from Arabia took Syria, Palestine and Egypt from the empire. Around the 670s, according to later chroniclers, a refugee from Syria named Kallinikos brought a liquid fire sprayed from tubes on ships, which burned even on the sea. The Byzantines called it sea fire or liquid fire; westerners later called it Greek fire. Its recipe is still unknown; modern scholars think it was probably based on crude oil. In the eighth century came the quarrel over holy images. Leo III's role may have been more limited than tradition claims; his son Constantine V called a council in 754 that condemned the veneration of icons. A council at Nicaea under the empress Irene restored them in 787, and after a second wave, they were restored for good in 843, still remembered in Orthodox churches as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Byzantine scribes copied Homer, Plato and Aristotle, and two brothers from Thessalonica, Cyril and Methodius, began the work that led to the Cyrillic alphabet.

Who were the Varangian Guard?

Basil II, who came into his full power in 976 and reigned, in name or in fact, for nearly fifty years, made an alliance around 988 with Vladimir, prince of Kiev, who married the emperor's sister Anna and accepted Christianity. From these northern warriors grew the Varangian Guard, the emperor's own bodyguard, with men from Scandinavia and the lands of the Rus, and later from England after 1066. On a marble parapet in the upper gallery of Hagia Sophia, faint Norse runes seem to contain the name Halfdan, most likely the graffiti of a Scandinavian visitor. The story that Basil blinded thousands of prisoners appears in later chronicles, and historians think it may have been exaggerated.

What happened in 1204 and 1453?

After the defeat at Manzikert in 1071, Alexios I Komnenos appealed to the West, one spark of the First Crusade; his daughter Anna Komnene wrote his history, the Alexiad. In April 1204 the Fourth Crusade, deep in debt to Venice, broke into Constantinople from the Golden Horn and plundered it for three days; the four bronze horses of the Hippodrome went to Venice, where copies stand on Saint Mark's and the originals are kept inside. The city was recovered in 1261, and in the early 1300s Theodore Metochites filled the Chora church with its mosaics and frescoes. In spring 1453 Sultan Mehmed II brought great cannon to the walls, and had dozens of ships hauled overland on greased wooden runways into the Golden Horn behind its chain. The city fell early on 29 May; the last emperor, Constantine XI, died in the fighting, and the tale of a marble emperor asleep beneath the Golden Gate is legend, not history. What lived on includes the Orthodox liturgy and icons, Justinian's laws, the Cyrillic alphabet and the Greek books that helped feed the Renaissance.
