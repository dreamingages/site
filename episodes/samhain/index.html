This sleep story follows Samhain, the old Gaelic festival at summer's end, and the long road that turned it into Halloween. It keeps a calm line between what the medieval tales, the archaeology and the folklore records actually show, and the newer ideas about Samhain that grew up in the last two centuries. There is no horror in it, no loud sounds and no ad breaks.

What is Samhain?

The oldest written Irish sources name four great days in the year: Imbolc at the start of February, Beltaine at the start of May, Lughnasa at the start of August, and Samain, now spelled Samhain, at the start of November, when summer was over and the dark half of the year began. In Modern Irish, Samhain (said softly, SOW-in) is still the ordinary name for November. Medieval scholars explained the word as sam fuin, "summer's end"; modern linguists think that is probably a learned guess, though it captures how the day felt.

Days in the early Celtic-speaking world seem to have been counted from nightfall. The Roman writer Julius Caesar noticed the Gauls measured time by nights. If that habit held in Ireland, the feast began on the evening before, which is why 31 October carries so much of the story.

What do the old Irish tales say about Samhain?

The tales were written down by Christian scholars, mostly in monasteries, between roughly the eighth and twelfth centuries. In them, Samain is the night when things happen: kings hold assemblies and feasts, tributes are paid, and the boundary with the Otherworld grows thin. In The Wasting Sickness of Cú Chulainn, the Ulster assembly lasts three days before Samain and three days after. In The Adventure of Nera, a young man at Cruachan follows the people of the hollow hills into their world. And in The Dream of Óengus, two swans fly to Newgrange singing a song so sweet that everyone who hears it sleeps for three days and three nights. What the tales do not clearly show is a festival devoted to the dead.

What have archaeologists found at Tlachtga and Tara?

Tlachtga, on the Hill of Ward near Athboy in County Meath, is ringed by four banks and ditches about 150 metres across. The story of a great Samhain fire there comes mostly from the historian Geoffrey Keating, writing in the 1630s. Excavations by University College Dublin between 2014 and 2016 found a late Bronze Age enclosure from about 1100 to 800 BCE, a new enclosure from around the fifth century CE that grew into the ringed monument still visible today, more than fifteen thousand fragments of animal bone, and a hoard of twenty-three Anglo-Saxon silver pennies. The site was clearly important for over two thousand years, but bones and ditches cannot tell us which day of the year people gathered. At Tara, the rising sun shines into the short passage of the Mound of the Hostages in early November and early February, though for many days around those dates, and the tomb is thousands of years older than the word Samain.

How did Samhain become Halloween?

A popular story says the Church moved All Saints' Day to 1 November to replace Samhain. The evidence is more tangled. The November date first appears clearly in England and the Frankish lands: Alcuin of York praised it around 800, and in the 830s Louis the Pious made it official in his realm. As the historian Ronald Hutton points out, Ireland seems to have adopted it later, not first. All Souls' Day on 2 November followed near the end of the tenth century, set by Odilo, abbot of Cluny. In English, 1 November became All Hallows' Day, the evening before All Hallows' Even, and by the eighteenth century the Scottish word Hallowe'en was in common use.

What are soul cakes, and where did trick or treat come from?

In parts of England, the poor went door to door at Hallowtide asking for soul cakes, small spiced breads or buns, and promised in return to pray for the souls of the household's dead. This was called souling, and it survived in some places, in song and in the bakery, long after its first purpose faded. In Scotland, guisers in disguise went house to house; one report from 1895 describes children carrying lanterns carved from turnips and receiving cakes, fruit and coins. The phrase's earliest known printed uses are Canadian: in November 1927, newspapers in High River and Blackie, Alberta, reported children demanding "trick or treat".

Was Samhain a Celtic New Year or a festival of the dead?

Those ideas are mostly modern. In the late eighteenth century, the antiquarian Charles Vallancey claimed Samhain was a god of death, but there is no evidence for such a god: Samhain is the name of a day and a month. John Rhys argued for a Celtic New Year, and James Frazer for a festival of the dead. Later historians, especially Hutton, found the medieval evidence for both thin, and the strongest link between this season and the dead comes from All Saints and All Souls. Today many modern pagans keep Samhain with sincerity, as a living festival that is new growth on very old ground.
